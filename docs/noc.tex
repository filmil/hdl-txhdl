\begin{abstract}
A router puts several peripherals behind one host. A network puts
several of both on a lattice and lets any of them reach any other. The
network here is nodes with a two-way link to each of their four
neighbours and a fifth, the exit, where something that is not a node
attaches; every link is two virtual channels; routing is dimension
order; and the exit is a bridge with AXI on one side and packets on
the other. Neither end knows the network is there: a host issues a
burst and awaits its answer as it would on a link, and a peripheral
accepts a transaction and answers it as it would.
\end{abstract}

\section{What a Link Carries}
\label{sec:n-pkt}

Figure~\ref{fig:n-lattice} is the shape of it. A link carries one beat
of one AXI channel, addressed to a node and stamped with the
node it came from.

\nocpart{pkt.rs}{pkt}{A packet.}{lst:n-pkt}

It is one shape, as wide as the widest beat needs, for the same reason
Razboj's instruction is~\cite{gpu}: every field a step reads has to
be at a fixed place, and a link that carried a header and then a body
would want a sequencer at both ends before anything else worked. A
read address beat leaves the data and the strobe at zero; a write
response leaves the address at zero; \code{Chan} says which fields
mean anything.

The source is carried rather than remembered. A node keeps no table of
who asked, and an answer finds its way home by reading the stamp the
question arrived with.

\section{Virtual Channels}
\label{sec:n-vc}

There are two, one for requests and one for responses, and they share
nothing: a node is a switch per virtual channel, and each switch has
its own buffers.

That is not tidiness, it is the thing that keeps the network from
seizing. A peripheral answers a request by sending a response. If a
full path of requests could hold up the responses behind it, the
responses that would empty that path could never leave, and the
network would stop with every buffer full and nothing able to move. On
two channels the response has a way through whatever the requests are
doing.

\section{A Node}
\label{sec:n-node}

Routing is dimension order: a packet goes east or west until it is in
its destination's column, then north or south until it is at its node,
then out of the exit.

\nocpart{switch.rs}{route}{The whole of the routing.}{lst:n-route}

On a lattice that is deadlock free with nothing else said, because a
packet never turns from Y back into X, so no cycle of waiting can
form among the links.

One packet moves through a switch per cycle. Each input's direction
and the room at the port it wants are worked out first, so that an
input whose port is full does not hold up the others, and the choice
among those that can move is by a fixed order that serves the four
links before the exit: a node does not starve the network in order to
feed itself. A fair arbiter would be the next thing to write; the
order here is a fixed priority and this document would rather say so
than not.

\nocpart{switch.rs}{run}{The switch, a cycle at a
time.}{lst:n-switch}

\nocpart{node.rs}{run}{A node: a switch per virtual channel. The
twenty ports are written out one by one because a lowered unit's
ports are a positional tuple and a named type for a side is
refused.}{lst:n-node}

\section{The Exit}
\label{sec:n-bridge}

A bridge, AXI on one side and packets on the other, in two halves.

The host half packs an address phase into a packet addressed by a map
of three ranges, whose last entry is the default route: give it a mask
of zero and every address that matched nothing else goes there. A
design that states its map that way has no address the network does
not know, which is why neither half has to answer for one.

A write of one beat is one packet, carrying its address phase and its
data together. That is what the hosts in this tree make, and it is
what keeps two of them from interleaving their write data at a
peripheral that has one port: AXI4 puts no identifier on the write
data channel, so a beat that arrived between another host's address
phase and its beat could not be told apart. A burst of more than one
beat wants a virtual channel per source, or reassembly at the
peripheral, and this bridge carries none.

So it refuses one. The host half takes the address phase of a longer
burst and every beat up to the one marked last, sends nothing onto the
network, and answers the host \texttt{SlvErr}. That is not what the
burst asked for, but it is an answer: before the refusal such a write
left as a single packet marked last, the peripheral's tracker waited
for a beat that had stayed behind on the host's channel, and the
client waited for ever. A host that asks the network for something it
cannot do now learns so in one burst's time, and the channel is left
clean for the next write. Carrying the burst is issue 125, and the
work that carries it is the work that takes the refusal out.

\nocpart{bridge.rs}{hostrun}{The host half.}{lst:n-host}

The peripheral half unpacks a request into the channels a
peripheral's tracker reads, and packs the answers back to the node
the request came from.

It also renames. An identifier belongs to the host that made it, so
two hosts may use the same one, and a peripheral behind one port
cannot tell their bursts apart; the second request would overwrite
the first's way home and one of the two answers would go to the wrong
corner of the lattice. The bridge therefore gives every request an
identifier of its own and remembers against it the node the request
came from and the identifier that node used. The answer comes back
under the local one and the packet goes home under the original. A
request waits when the identifier whose turn it is has not been
answered, which is what bounds how many bursts a peripheral has in
flight.

\nocpart{bridge.rs}{perrun}{The peripheral half.}{lst:n-per}

That renaming is not a refinement. It is the difference between the
network working and not, and the test that found it is the one below
that puts two hosts on one memory.

\section{What Is Checked}
\label{sec:n-tests}

Three tests, all driving the whole of it: four nodes, both bridges,
both trackers and memory, with a host client that issues and awaits
as it would on a link.

The first sends a burst from one corner of a two by two lattice to the
far one, so that it crosses in X and then in Y, and reads back what it
wrote. The second puts a host at two corners and one memory at a
third, each host writing its own word and reading it back. Nothing
keeps a table of who asked, so the second test is the source stamp and
the renaming being right or the test failing, and before the renaming
was written it did fail.

The third puts four peripherals behind one node. An exit holds one
bridge, and the bridge's other side is five ordinary AXI channels, so
a router of four goes there, with a memory in each of its ranges. Two
hosts write a word into every peripheral, read each back, and read an
address that the network sends to that node but that no peripheral
has. Each word has to be in its own memory and in no other, and the
router has to answer the missing address with \code{DecErr} itself.
A peripheral behind the router needs nothing of the network: the
router does not know a bridge is in front of it, and the bridge does
not know a router is behind it.


\section{Four Corners}
\label{sec:n-soc}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.52\columnwidth]{soc_scene.png}
\caption{An icosahedron. The core worked it out and wrote it into
memory as a display list; the rasteriser read the list out of that
memory and drew it there, two hops away from the core.}
\label{fig:n-soc}
\end{figure}

A core at 0,0, a GPU at 1,0, a memory at 0,1 and a serial port at 1,1:
two hosts and two peripherals, none of which knows that the others are
there or that the network is. The core is Vreteno running a program
compiled for it; the GPU is Razboj, the rasteriser; the memory is the
simulation-only \code{Ram}; the serial port is the same \code{Uart}
the core has always had, with a terminal on its lines.

The two hosts work together through the memory and through nothing
else. The program holds an icosahedron as twelve vertices and twenty
faces, turns it to the view, throws away the faces that point away
from the camera, shades the rest by how squarely they face a light,
and writes each as a triangle of the display list at \code{0x2000},
with the count at \code{0x4000} last. The rasteriser has been reading
that count since the first cycle. When it turns non-zero, the
rasteriser fetches the list and draws it into the framebuffer at
\code{0x8000}. The program's constants and its stack are at
\code{0x1000}, and the serial port is at \code{0x3000}, where the core
says \code{icosahedron} when it is done.

\socpart{map}{The address map, stated in a host bridge's type. Both
hosts use it: the serial port, the program's data, and then everything
else, the list and the framebuffer included.}{lst:n-map}

The masks cover the whole address. A mask of \code{0xf000} compares
only the digit the base is written in, and the framebuffer's last
rows, at \code{0x13000} and above, then went to the serial port: the
picture came out of the terminal as a line of the background colour's
low byte.

Each corner is the same three pieces: the client, an \code{AxiHost} or
an \code{AxiPer}, and the bridge. Nothing was added to the core to put
it here. The rasteriser is told three addresses in its type, the
framebuffer's base, the list's and the count's, and works its
addresses out at the link's width, since a framebuffer above
\code{0x10000} would otherwise wrap onto whatever the map has below.

The program found a fault in the core. A multiply whose operand is
the word the instruction before it loaded latched the register's old
value, because the load was still waiting for its answer from the bus
and the sequencer started anyway. The colour of every face came out
with no red in it. The core's lockstep test now has that case, and the
sequencer waits for the answer.

\label{sec:n-cost}

\toprule

\midrule
The core writes the list & & 13\,191 \\
The GPU draws the list & 36\,516 & 78\,391 \\
\bottomrule

The demonstration prints both numbers for the GPU. It hands the list
the core wrote to the rasteriser and its framebuffer joined by a
plain link, draws it again, and checks that the picture is the same.
On the lattice the rasteriser takes a little over twice as long,
counted from the cycle the count is written.

That is the cost of throughput rather than of latency. A hop adds
about as much to one transaction's round trip as it does to the next,
and a host that waits for each answer, as the core does for its loads,
pays that and nothing more. The rasteriser writes over twelve thousand
pixels and never waits for any of them, so what it pays is the
network's rate: one packet through a node per cycle, and a write is a
packet each way across two hops.

The rate is the number to improve, and improving it is arbitration and
width rather than routing: a switch that moved a packet per port per
cycle rather than one per node would carry the rasteriser's stream at
the rate the link does.

\subsection{What a node's own logic costs}
\label{sec:n-timing}

How fast a node can be clocked was read off the logic for a long time
and not measured. It has been measured now, with the hermetic Vivado
the rest of the tree uses: \code{//lib/parts:node\_synth} and
\code{//lib/parts:switch\_synth} put one node, and one switch inside
it, through synthesis for the Artix-7 the board has. Both are manual,
as every Vivado target here is, and both take about a minute.

A node holds no register. The flip-flops that bound its path are the
channels' on either side of it, and those belong to whatever
instantiates the node, so the question is the node's own delay from an
input to an output; the constraint asks for that rather than for a
clock period. Table~\ref{tab:n-timing} is the answer.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\caption{One node, and one switch inside it, through synthesis for
the \code{xc7a200tfbg484-2}. The delay is from an input pad to an
output pad, so it carries an input buffer and an output buffer,
3.23~ns of the total, which a node inside a design does not pay.}
\label{tab:n-timing}
\centering\footnotesize
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrr@{}}
\toprule
 & switch & node \\
\midrule
Look-up tables & 266 & 534 \\
Registers & 0 & 0 \\
Longest path, pad to pad & 7.73~ns & 7.61~ns \\
Of that, the two pads & 3.23~ns & 3.23~ns \\
Levels of logic between them & 5 & 5 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Five levels of look-up table is the whole of it, and the same five in
a node as in a switch, since a node is two switches that do not chain.
At the tenth of a nanosecond a LUT costs on this part, that is about
half a nanosecond of logic; the rest of the measured delay is the two
pads and an unplaced estimate of the wires. So a node has room at
100~MHz, and the shortening that was proposed for it, carrying the
next hop's direction in the packet so that a node reads its direction
rather than working it out, is not worth doing yet.

The path is not the one that was expected, and that is worth writing
down. It runs from an output's \code{ready} to another output's
\code{valid}, which is the backpressure chain through the arbitration,
not the wide multiplexer on the packet. A switch that wanted to be
faster would shorten that chain first.

The two hardly contend. While the core works, the rasteriser only
polls the count, one read at a time; by the time it draws, the core
has halted. A program that kept writing while the GPU drew would
contend for longer, and the fixed priority in a switch, which serves
the four links before the exit, is the thing to watch when it does.

\section{What Is Not Here}
\label{sec:n-not}

The lattice is wired by a helper that makes the channels and hands out
the ends, which does not lower; a design that is to be synthesised
writes the same wiring as a unit of units and lets the lowering make
the netlist. Bursts of more than one beat do not cross the network,
and are refused rather than carried.
The arbiter in a switch is a fixed priority. A switch moves one packet
per cycle rather than one per port, which is what the rasteriser's
stream pays for above.
