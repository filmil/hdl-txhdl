\section{A Struct of Ports That Holds Structs of Ports}
\label{sec:x-nested}

A struct of ports could not hold another: each field had to be one
port, an \code{In}, an \code{Out}, a \code{Tx}, an \code{Rx} or a
\code{Pad}. So a design that took a host's AXI4 pins as part of a
larger side wrote the pins out one by one, each under a prefix, and
packed them back into the host's own struct by hand where it passed
them on.

A field may now be a struct of ports itself. Its ports are the
netlist's under the field's name: the \code{left} link of the
\code{Links} below is \code{left\_aw}, \code{left\_ar} and the rest,
and \code{right}'s likewise. In a body, every name of a chain up to a
method call is part of the port's, so \code{links.left.aw.peek()}
reads the port \code{left\_aw}; a port has no fields of its own to
read without one. Passed whole to a child, a side joins each nested
struct whole, in the order the struct declares it.

The nesting works at every level. \code{Links} is declared in this
file, and each of its fields is \code{LitePort}, declared in
\code{txhdl\_parts}. \code{Links} derives \code{Ports} as well, so a
unit in another crate could take it, and the run checks the ports the
derive lists: the two links', flattened, in order.

\code{Mailbox} is a unit of units that takes both links as one side
and passes it on whole, and the word behind them reads through it.
The run is \code{ex\_bundle}'s. The build checks the netlist of
\code{Mailbox} against it under nvc and Verilator.
