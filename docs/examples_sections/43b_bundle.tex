\section{A Struct of Ports from Another Crate}
\label{sec:x-bundle}

The structs of the last section are read from the unit's own file.
A struct that many units share is declared once, somewhere else,
and possibly in another crate, whose source the compiler of this one
never sees. Such a struct derives \code{Ports}, and that is all
\code{\#[lower]} asks of it. The lowering reads each \code{side.f}
of the body as a port. The derive says what each port is, its kind,
width, clock and the layout of its value, when \code{lowered} runs.

\code{LitePort} is such a struct, declared in \code{txhdl\_parts}
beside the AXI-Lite beats: the five channels a peripheral holds on a
link. \code{Shared} is one word behind two links, \code{left} and
\code{right}, as a mailbox between two processors would be. Both
sides have the same type, so a port is named for its side as well as
its field. \code{left.aw} is the port \code{left\_aw} and
\code{right.r} is \code{right\_r}, each with the usual
\code{\_data}, \code{\_valid} and \code{\_ready}. A write from both
sides in one cycle keeps the left one's word.

A parent that passes such a struct to a child writes a struct
literal, \code{LitePort \{ aw: x, .. \}}. It sees the fields and not
the child's side, so each field joins the one port of the child
whose name ends in \code{\_aw}. Two such ports on one child are
refused with both names. A unit of units may take such a struct
as a side of its own and pass its fields to its children one by one,
as the DDR3 peripheral passes its link to its bridge; passing the
whole side on is refused, since its fields cannot be seen.

The run writes on each side and reads on the other, then writes on
both at once and reads the word back from both. Every read is
checked against Rust. The netlist is checked against this run under
both simulators, at its ports.
